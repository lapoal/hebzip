The Barcelonnettes (Spanish: los barcelonnettes, also spelled barcelonettes) were emigrants from the Ubaye Valley around Barcelonnette, in the Alpes-de-Haute-Provence of southeast France. They settled in Mexico from the early 1800s and opened many of the country's first department stores, then called almacenes de novedades (novelty stores). By the early 1900s they ran 70% of Mexico's textile factories and its biggest stores.
This article covers the stores from about 1820 to 1960, by region. El Palacio de Hierro, El Puerto de Liverpool, La Ciudad de Londres and Las Fábricas Universales have their own articles and are only summarized here; the modern Palacio and Liverpool chains are a separate story.
New arrivals started as employees, with food and lodging but no pay in the first year; later they opened their own stores with help from earlier emigrants. The stores followed the Paris department-store model. In the First World War, 561 employees of the stores and mills went back to fight for France; El Centro Mercantil alone sent 47.
The owners pooled their money to supply their own shelves. The Compañía Industrial de Orizaba (CIDOSA) was formed on 28 June 1889 by merchants almost all from Barcelonnette, including the owners of El Palacio de Hierro, El Puerto de Liverpool, La Ciudad de Londres, El Puerto de Veracruz and El Correo Francés; its mill was at Río Blanco. In 1896 another group formed the Compañía Industrial Veracruzana (CIVSA), with a mill at Santa Rosa, 11 km from Orizaba.
Mexico City was the center of the network. Trentini's 1907 descriptions of the grandes tiendas (large stores) cover El Palacio de Hierro, El Puerto de Liverpool, El Centro Mercantil and Las Fábricas Universales.

The store opened on the Zócalo on 2 September 1899, in a building by Daniel Garza, although Galán Amaro dates the firm to 1884. The stained-glass ceiling by Jacques Grüber was not finished until 1908, and the store kept operating until 1958. The building became the Gran Hotel Ciudad de México in 1968.
This is the Mexico City store, not the later Fábricas de Francia chain of Guadalajara. Joseph Tron started there as an employee after arriving in 1864. Tron and Léautaud bought it from Gassier and Reynaud; Galán Amaro dates the store to 1842 and their purchase to 1880.
La Valenciana was one of the two big Barcelonnette houses in the capital. Sébastien Robert partnered with Émile Meyran there, and the two later founded El Centro Mercantil. Proal and Charpenel call Meyran a director and partner of both houses. Joseph Léautaud worked there for ten years before founding El Palacio de Hierro. Galán Amaro's table names Aubert, Meyran, Plauchud, Paulino Richaud and Robert as owners (1884).
Galán Amaro's table dates La Francia Marítima to 1851 and lists Jean-Baptiste Ebrard, Félix Fortolis, Adrien Jean and Luis Veyan as partners. Trentini gave 1851 and the names Ebrard and Fortoul for El Puerto de Liverpool.
Galán Amaro's table lists El Correo Francés under Mario Lambert and Alexandre Reynaud, and her text says Reynaud set the store up in Mexico City. Lambert was also one of the original CIDOSA investors.
A large partnership owned La Reforma del Comercio. Galán Amaro's table lists Emile André, Emile and Joseph Audiffred, Faudon, Auguste Garcin, Jules Lions and Adolphe Richaud; Proal and Charpenel call Garcin one of its directors and Richaud, who arrived in Mexico in 1870, one of its owners.
París-Londres had stores in Mexico City, Guadalajara and Querétaro; descendants of its founder, Couzin, ran it in the late twentieth century. The source does not say where Couzin was from.

Las Fábricas de Francia, opened in 1878, was Guadalajara's first department store. Trentini says Fortoul, Bec y Cía. founded it, with a knitwear factory of more than 300 workers. Branches later opened in León, Morelia, Tepic, Mazatlán and Aguascalientes.
La Ciudad de Londres in Guadalajara was founded by Lébre and Dandoulf. Trentini puts it on the corner of López Cotilla street and Corona avenue; Valerio Ulloa dates it to between 1851 and 1858 and puts it at 16 de Septiembre and Juárez.
La Ciudad de México in Guadalajara was founded in 1880 by Adrien Berlie and Eugène Cogordan, who that year handed it to Louis Gas and Félix Cogordan. The source does not say whether any of them was a Barcelonnette.
In Morelia, Aimé Tron bought the novelty store Al Progreso in 1911; it got its goods from El Palacio de Hierro in Mexico City. When the lease ended in 1932, the group opened Al Sombrero Colorado, a novelty and hat store, in the neighboring Tron Hermanos building. A 1907 list by Auguste Genin also names Barcelonnette stores in Guanajuato and León.
Gamboa Ojeda counts ten clothing and novelty houses in Puebla during the Porfiriato, plus three hat shops and a shirt shop; not all were founded by Barcelonnettes.
It was the oldest and biggest of the ten, founded in 1862 by Juan Bautista Lions and Juan Bautista Chaix. Proal and Charpenel say the founders were the Lions brothers Eugène, Jules and Antoine, sons of a Barcelonnette tailor. From 1862 to 1910 it was on the ground floor of the Casa de los Muñecos; its new building opened on 21 February 1910. Gamboa Ojeda ties the design of that building to the Paris house La Samaritaine. Adrián Reynaud joined in 1880, and after the Lions family left in 1911 he carried on with León Signoret until 1918.
Al Puerto de Liverpool was the third most important French clothing store in Puebla. It was in a central building, first rented and then bought, and never had one built for it. José Desdier founded it in 1874; Julio Caire, Luis Chaix and the brothers Julio and Eugenio Sibilot joined him later.
La Primavera was the second biggest, but other foreign merchants founded it in 1880, not Barcelonnettes. Paulino Richaud, Juan Robert and Eugenio Caire took it over in the early 1900s; Galán Amaro gives 1903.
Las Fábricas de Francia began in the 1890s as a branch of J. Ebrard y Compañía of Mexico City. Julio A. Lions later owned it; in 1902 he handed it over in payment to El Palacio de Hierro, whose manager Fortunato Béraud then ran it with Javier Philip and José Proal.
Julio A. Lions founded Au Bon Marché in the 1890s, and in 1895 it took over the stock, credits and debts of La Independencia. Lions's former partners bought it in 1903, the date Galán Amaro gives for the firm.
Alfredo Esmenjaud and the brothers Isidoro and Santiago Couttolenc founded El Louvre in 1900. La Barata was the smallest of the ten. A merchant who was neither French nor Barcelonnette founded it, and the Lions brothers and Julio Rebattu bought it in 1903.
Al Puerto de Veracruz also had a store in Puebla, at the corner of Avenida 4 Poniente and Calle 5 de Mayo in the Centro Histórico. The building dates from the 1950s and was one of the first modern buildings in the center; it had underground parking on the 4 Poniente side. A local news report says Spanish businessmen based in Veracruz ran the store, and it does not connect it to the Barcelonnette owners of the Mexico City store of the same name.
The Gran Sombrerería Francesa was begun by Barthélemy Rebattu and later run under the name of Léon-Baptiste Esmenjaud. Galán Amaro's table of firms owned by Barcelonnettes also lists these Puebla stores:
La Independencia (1851), Honorato Arnaud, Léon Donneaud, Jules Lions and Paulino Richaud.
El Incendio, Isidore Couttolenc, Enrique Gislard and the Manuel brothers.
