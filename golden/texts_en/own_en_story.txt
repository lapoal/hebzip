The Last Bus

The last bus from the valley leaves at ten past eleven, and everybody in the village knows it. Miss Harrow, who ran the post office for thirty years, used to say that you could set a clock by its lights coming round the bend by the mill.

On the night of the first snow, the bus did not come. At eleven twenty the three people waiting at the stop - a nurse going home from her shift, a boy with a guitar case, and an old man carrying a cake in a paper box - looked at each other and said nothing. At half past, the boy took out his phone, but there was no signal so far up the road.

"It will come," said the old man. "It always comes."

The nurse laughed, not unkindly. "Everything always comes, until the day it doesn't."

They waited. The snow fell slowly, in large soft flakes, and the cake box began to turn white. The boy opened his case, took out the guitar, and played something quiet with gloves on, missing every third note. Nobody minded.

At ten to midnight, two yellow lights came round the bend by the mill. The driver opened the door and apologised: a tree had fallen across the lower road, and he had waited for the farmers to drag it away.

The old man climbed in first. He set the cake on his knees, brushed the snow from the lid, and said to no one in particular, "There. I told you."
